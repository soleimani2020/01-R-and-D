Who Will Repay a Loan? Predicting Risk and Making the Approval Decision

Scenario.

A small lender reviews loan applications one by one. The two possible mistakes are not equally costly.

(a) Approving a borrower who later defaults can lose most of the principal (say 5k on a 10k loan).

(b) Turning away a borrower who would have repaid only forgoes the interest.

One bad approval can wipe out the profit from several good loans.

The lender therefore needs:

  1. A risk score that ranks applicants well, so likely defaulters sit at the top.
  2. Trustworthy probabilities, so a predicted 10% default risk really means about 10 in 100 such borrowers default.
  3. An approve/reject rule that reflects these unequal costs. Instead of the usual 50% cutoff, reject an applicant when the expected cost of approving is higher than the expected cost of rejecting. With a 5-to-1 cost ratio, that means rejecting anyone whose default risk is above about 17%.

Questions

  1. How well can the application data rank applicants by default risk?
  2. Are the predicted probabilities reliable enough to act on?
  3. Which approval cut-off minimizes expected cost, and how much better is it than the naive 50% rule?
  4. Which application details drive the score, and does the rule treat age groups very differently?

Assumed costs (stated up front, because they decide the answer): approving a loan that defaults costs 5 units; rejecting an applicant who would have repaid costs 1 unit. This 5-to-1 ratio is the one published with the German Credit dataset; change it in the setup cell to see how the decision changes.

Data: the public German Credit dataset (1,000 past applicants, 20 recorded attributes, outcome good/bad), available from the UCI repository and OpenML. Put a copy named german_credit.csv next to this notebook, or let the notebook fetch it from OpenML.

5. Candidate models

All candidate models share one preprocessing recipe. It sits inside each pipeline, so it is re-fitted on every training fold and never sees the test data. Numbers are scaled and categories are one-hot encoded. A do-nothing baseline sets the floor, and every other model should beat it.

In [27]
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline

# Split columns into numbers and categories
num_cols = X.select_dtypes(include="number").columns
cat_cols = X.select_dtypes(exclude="number").columns

# Preprocessing pipeline: scale the numbers, one-hot encode the categories
prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", StandardScaler(), num_cols),
    ("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), cat_cols),
])

# Models to compare, each goes through the preprocessing
candidates = {
    "Baseline": Pipeline([("prep", prep), ("model", DummyClassifier(strategy="prior"))]),
    "Logistic regression": Pipeline([("prep", prep), ("model", LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000))]),
    "Random forest": Pipeline([("prep", prep), ("model", RandomForestClassifier(random_state=SEED))]),
    "Gradient boosting": Pipeline([("prep", prep), ("model", HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=SEED))]),
}

6. Compare by how well they rank risk

With only 750 training applicants, a single test score can be misleading, because it depends on how the applicants happen to be split. To get a steadier result, I split the data into 5 parts (keeping the default rate at about 30% in each part), test on each part in turn, and repeat the whole process 3 times with different splits. That gives 15 test scores per model, and I use their average.

I judge each model in three ways:

  • ROC AUC: if I pick one applicant who defaulted and one who repaid, how often does the defaulter get the higher risk score? 1.0 is perfect, 0.5 is guessing.
  • Average precision: when I look at the highest-scored applicants, how many of them are real defaulters? Higher is better.
  • Brier score: how close are the predicted probabilities to what really happened? Lower is better.

The first two measure how well the model ranks applicants. The Brier score measures whether the probabilities can be trusted.

In [28]
cv = RepeatedStratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, n_repeats=3, random_state=SEED)
scoring = {"auc": "roc_auc", "ap": "average_precision", "brier": "neg_brier_score"}

rows = {}
for name, est in candidates.items():
    s = cross_validate(est, X_train, y_train, cv=cv, scoring=scoring)
    rows[name] = {
                  "ROC AUC": s["test_auc"].mean(),
                  "AUC spread": s["test_auc"].std(),
                  "Avg precision": s["test_ap"].mean(),
                  "Brier": -s["test_brier"].mean()
                  }

cv_table = pd.DataFrame(rows).T.sort_values("ROC AUC", ascending=False)
best_name = cv_table.index[0]
display(cv_table)

7. Can the probabilities be trusted?

A lender acts on the probability itself ("this applicant has a 30% chance of default"), so the check is whether predicted risk matches observed frequency. I use out-of-fold predictions on the training applicants, so no applicant is scored by a model that saw them.

I compare the three real models (logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting) in one plot. Applicants are sorted into 8 groups of equal size by predicted risk. For each group, the plot shows the average predicted risk (x-axis) against the share who actually defaulted (y-axis). The dashed diagonal marks perfect calibration.

  • A line close to the diagonal means the probabilities can be trusted.
  • A line below the diagonal means the model is too pessimistic (it predicts more risk than occurs).
  • A line above the diagonal means the model is too optimistic (it predicts less risk than occurs), which is the costly mistake.

The region around 10 to 25% predicted risk matters most, because the approval cut-off of about 17% sits there.

8. Where should the approval cut-off be?

Rejecting everyone above 50% predicted risk ignores the costs: approving a borrower who defaults costs 5 units, while rejecting one who would have repaid costs 1 unit. I therefore try every cut-off from 5% to 95% and add up the total cost of both mistakes. I choose the cut-off on the training applicants (using out-of-fold probabilities), then check it once on the 250 test applicants, which were not used for any earlier decision. The theory predicts a cut-off of about 17% (1 in 6), and I compare it with the naive 50% rule.

In [30]
final = candidates["Logistic regression"]

def total_cost(y_true, p, cutoff):
    y_true = np.asarray(y_true)
    reject = p >= cutoff
    approved_bad = ((~reject) & (y_true == 1)).sum()    # approved, then defaulted
    rejected_good = (reject & (y_true == 0)).sum()      # rejected, would have repaid
    return COST_BAD_APPROVED * approved_bad + COST_GOOD_REJECTED * rejected_good

# 1. Find the best cut-off on the training data
oof = cross_val_predict(final, X_train, y_train, cv=cv5, method="predict_proba")[:, 1]
cutoffs = np.arange(0.05, 0.96, 0.01)
costs = [total_cost(y_train, oof, c) for c in cutoffs]
best_cutoff = cutoffs[np.argmin(costs)]
print(f"Best cut-off on training data: {best_cutoff:.2f}  (theory: {1/6:.2f})")

plt.plot(cutoffs, costs)
plt.axvline(best_cutoff, color="k", linestyle="--")
plt.xlabel("Reject if predicted risk is above this cut-off")
plt.ylabel("Total cost (training data)")
plt.show()

# 2. Check on the test applicants, which were not used so far
final.fit(X_train, y_train)
p_test = final.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]

for label, c in [("Naive 50% rule", 0.50), ("Theory cut-off (1/6)", 1/6), ("Best cut-off", best_cutoff)]:
    cost = total_cost(y_test, p_test, c)
    print(f"{label:22s} cut-off {c:.2f}   total cost {cost:4d}   per applicant {cost/len(y_test):.2f}")

9. Final check on the held-out applicants

So far, every choice (model, calibration check, cut-off) was made on the training applicants. This section checks the result once on the 250 test applicants, whom the model has never seen.

The chosen model is fitted on all training data and scored on the test applicants. I compare four policies by total cost, using the same costs as before (5 units for approving a borrower who defaults, 1 unit for rejecting one who would have repaid):

  • Approve everyone: no model, nobody is rejected.
  • Reject everyone: no model, nobody gets a loan.
  • Model with the naive 50% rule: reject only if predicted risk is above 50%.
  • Model with the chosen cut-off (1/6): reject if predicted risk is above about 17%.

The model is only worth using if the last policy is cheaper than all three others.

10. What drives the score, and does age matter?

Two last checks answer the final question from the introduction.

Which details drive the score? I use permutation importance on the test applicants: I shuffle one application detail at a time and measure how much the ROC AUC drops. A big drop means the model relies on that detail; a drop near zero means it barely matters.

Does the rule treat age groups very differently? I split the test applicants into four age bands (under 25, 25-34, 35-49, 50+). For each band I compare the share the rule rejects with the share that actually defaulted. Some difference is expected, since groups can differ in real risk, but a much higher reject rate than the default rate justifies would be a warning sign. Each band is small, so only large gaps should be taken seriously.

11. Conclusions

This notebook asked how a small lender can decide whether to approve loan applications when approving a borrower who defaults costs five times as much as turning away a good one. The answers to the four questions are below.

1. How well can the application data rank applicants by default risk? Reasonably well, but far from perfectly. In repeated cross-validation, logistic regression reached a ROC AUC of 0.774 and random forest 0.768, against 0.500 for the do-nothing baseline. Gradient boosting was slightly lower (0.752). The differences between the three models are smaller than the fold-to-fold spread (about 0.03), so they are effectively tied.

2. Are the predicted probabilities reliable enough to act on? Logistic regression's probabilities were the most trustworthy, staying close to the diagonal, including around the 17% cut-off. Gradient boosting was too optimistic at low predicted risk, which is the costly direction for a lender. Random forest was erratic. Each calibration point rests on only about 94 applicants, so small wiggles are noise. I therefore chose logistic regression, which is also the simplest model to explain.

3. Which cut-off minimizes expected cost, and how much better is it than 50%? The cost ratio of 5 to 1 gives a theoretical cut-off of 1/6 (about 17%). The best cut-off found on the training data was 0.20, and the cost curve is flat between about 0.10 and 0.20, so the exact value is not critical. On the 250 held-out applicants:

Policy Total cost Per applicant

The chosen rule is about 31% cheaper than the naive 50% rule. The naive rule is even worse than rejecting everyone, so a good ranking model only pays off when its cut-off reflects the real costs.

4. Which details drive the score, and does the rule treat age groups differently? Permutation importance on the test applicants shows that the model relies most on checking-account status (AUC drop 0.094), followed by loan duration (0.045), loan purpose (0.034), savings status (0.030) and credit history (0.029). Loan amount matters less (0.013). This fits the first look at the data: about 49% of applicants with an overdrawn account defaulted, against about 12% of those with no checking account, and defaulters tended to have longer and somewhat larger loans.

The rule rejects about 60% of applicants, twice the 30% default rate, because a cost-aware cut-off is deliberately cautious. Reject rates follow real risk: highest for applicants under 25 (72% rejected, 40% default) and lowest for ages 35-49 (49% rejected, 19% default). The gap between reject rate and default rate is about 30 percentage points in the three largest age bands, so no group is treated much more harshly than its risk implies. The 50+ band has only 22 applicants and is too noisy to interpret.

Recommendation Use logistic regression and reject applicants whose predicted default risk is above about 17%. The simple 1/6 rule performs as well as the tuned cut-off, so it needs no extra search.

Limitations - Small data. There are 1,000 applicants (250 in the test set), so the exact savings are approximate. The order of the policies is clear, but the percentages could shift with a different split. - Assumed costs. The 5-to-1 ratio comes with the dataset. A real lender should use its own figures for losses and interest, since the cut-off follows directly from them. - Old, selected data. The data are historical German loans, and outcomes are only known for applicants who were approved, so the model may not generalize to people the bank would have rejected. - Cost of caution. The rule rejects many applicants who would have repaid. Its cost counts only the forgone interest, so it should be compared with the lender's actual margins. - Fairness. Age is an input to the model. This notebook only checks outcomes across age groups, and using age in real lending decisions is restricted or illegal in many countries.
